US equities & earnings

AI debut cannot stop a broad US equity selloff

US equities finished November 19 sharply lower: the S&P 500 fell 2.02% to 7,175.83, the Dow dropped 2.19% to 49,097.06, and the Nasdaq Composite lost 2.25% to 23,879.20.[1] Anthropic ended its debut 17.1% above its offer price, but the S&P recorded its sixth consecutive declining session.[1] Nvidia fell 3.3%, while Advanced Micro Devices dropped 7.7%; the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index lost 2.4%.[1] Palo Alto Networks provided a counterexample, advancing 6.4% after raising its revenue outlook.[1] The broader technology sector ended down 1.9%.[1]

Context & sources

Why it matters

Interpretation: one successful listing is not evidence that broad risk appetite recovered; cybersecurity gains contrast with semiconductor losses.[1]

Confidence: High

  • [1] Reuters · US close

Walmart raises guidance, but investors mark shares lower

Walmart reported fiscal third-quarter revenue of $188.8 billion, up 5.2%, with GAAP earnings of $0.62 per share and adjusted earnings of $0.65.[2] The company lifted fiscal 2027 guidance to constant-currency net-sales growth of 4.9%–5.2% and adjusted EPS of $2.84–$2.89.[2] Reported operating income rose 16.0%, versus 5.5% growth on an adjusted, constant-currency basis.[2] Nevertheless, Reuters reported that Walmart shares slipped 1.0% in Thursday's session.[1]

Context & sources

Why it matters

Interpretation: raised management guidance need not exceed investor expectations; reported and adjusted profit growth are different accounting comparisons.[1][2]

Confidence: High

  • [2] Walmart · FY27 Q3

BIS extends chip controls to overseas remote computing

BIS’s interim final rule, effective November 19, targets significant remote compute supplied by overseas data centers to entities headquartered in Country Group D:5 or Macau.[20] It adds due diligence and licensing for specified advanced-chip shipments to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, with qualifying US-headquartered recipients exempt subject to reporting.[20] Commercial recommender systems are excluded from the remote-compute requirement; the rule also formally rescinds the earlier AI Diffusion Rule.[20]

Context & sources

Why it matters

Interpretation: operators must examine customers and ultimate access, not merely the server’s location; revenue effects are not established.[20]

Confidence: High on published rule; not transaction-specific legal advice

  • [20] BIS · remote compute rule

SK hynix’s SC26 account describes MARS rack architecture, CXL memory and DRAM/SSD hybrid memory demonstrations for shared KV caches.[21] It also discusses CPU-server request and token sharing, Compute-using-DRAM, Custom HBM Array concepts and HBM4E/HBM4 exhibits.[21] These are company-described demonstrations, not independently reproduced benchmarks or blanket general-availability announcements.[21]

AWS review separates GPU isolation from utilization economics

AWS’s November 19 review describes managed NVIDIA Dynamo on HyperPod, including disaggregated prefill/decode and KV-aware routing; it consolidates changes across months, not solely new same-day launches.[22] MIG partitions dedicate accelerator compute and memory but still share host CPU, host memory, networking and storage; billing remains the full instance-hour.[22]
